# Do Sudden Changes in Demand Matter? Forecasting and Stock Planning with Walmart Data

[![tests](../../actions/workflows/tests.yml/badge.svg)](../../actions/workflows/tests.yml)

A shop has to guess how much of each product it will sell, so it keeps enough on the shelf without paying for stock
that sits there. Sometimes a product's sales change for good: it gets more popular, a competitor turns up, the price
moves. We wanted to know three things. Do these lasting changes really happen, and can we spot them? If we update
the forecast after spotting one, does it get better? And what does it all mean for how much stock to keep?

We use Walmart's public M5 data: daily sales of every product in its California stores from 2011 to 2016. We model
the 726 products that sell a lot and fairly steadily, and check the main results again on Texas and Wisconsin.

## Final results

We compared 21 forecasting methods on the same test: forecast 28 days ahead, six times in a row, from December 2015 to
May 2016. The methods range from "copy last week" to classical statistics (Holt-Winters, SARIMA), machine learning
(XGBoost, LightGBM) and neural networks (LSTM, GRU, PatchTST). No method saw the test period before it was scored.
This final comparison uses a sample of 150 of the 726 products. All numbers are units sold per product per day.

| Method | Average miss (units/day) | Compared with copying last week |
|---|---|---|
| XGBoost (tuned) | 3.41 | 28% smaller miss, ranked 1st of 21 |
| Average of the top 3 | 3.49 | 2nd |
| Stack of several models | 3.46 | 3rd |
| Combination (SARIMAX, Holt-Winters, Theta) | 3.50 | 4th |
| SARIMA | 3.52 | 6th |
| Neural networks (GRU, PatchTST, LSTM) | 4.19 to 4.49 | worse than every statistical method |
| Copy last week (baseline) | 4.72 | |

What this tells us:

1. **Machine learning on a good set of features wins, but only narrowly over the classical models.** XGBoost is
   clearly better than copying last week (99% of products improve, p < 0.001). Against SARIMA and the Combination
   the gap is small and not statistically proven (p = 0.052 and 0.20), so the safe reading is that the best classical
   models and XGBoost are close.
2. **Neural networks did not pay off here.** With about five years of daily history per product, the simpler models
   were more accurate and far quicker to fit.
3. **Better stock decisions come from honest error ranges.** With a 95% target and a 7-day lead time, reordering with
   XGBoost forecasts and error ranges measured from past mistakes cost 13% less than copying last week and 40% less
   than the textbook safety-stock formula, while still filling 99% of demand.
4. **Lasting shifts in demand are real (about 63% of busy products show one), but retraining after a shift did not
   improve forecasts** for any model tried.

Storage and lost-sale costs are our assumptions, not Walmart's figures. The full tables are on the dashboard's
**Final leaderboard**, **Forecast viewer**, **Volatility (GARCH)** and **Stock impact, best model** pages.

### What we found

1. Do sales levels really change? Yes, often. After removing changes that would appear just by chance, about 63% of the busy products changed their normal sales level at some point. Our change detector only notices big changes, though: a sudden 20% jump is spotted just 18% of the time.
2. Which forecast works best? Combination, ahead of SARIMA, the runner-up, for 65% of products. Its typical error is 23% smaller than simply copying last week's sales. It guesses a little low overall (1.6% under).
3. Does retraining the model after a change help? No. We tried 2 different ways. None made forecasts better, and the simplest one made them slightly worse. The same was true in Texas and Wisconsin.
4. Does a better forecast save money on stock? Partly. Using the better forecast to decide when to reorder means holding 9% less stock at 5% higher cost than the simple method, and it was clearly cheaper in 1 of 3 time periods tested. Setting safety stock from realistic error ranges cut the cost by 24%. These costs use assumed prices for storage and lost sales, not real Walmart figures.

### Classical time-series models

On a sample of 150 products, with the same backtest as above: 17% of the 726 products test as stationary (ADF and KPSS agree). SARIMA (median MASE 0.755) and Holt-Winters (0.757) against seasonal naive (0.973): SARIMA better: Yes (median difference -0.223, 95% CI -0.237 to -0.200); Holt-Winters better: Yes (-0.218, 95% CI -0.233 to -0.207). The lowest median error is Holt-Winters (damped, Box-Cox) (0.744). Reacting to a detected break helps SARIMA: No (restart +0.0000, level-shift dummy +0.0000 MASE, n = 75 products). ### Time-series models

Eight time-series contenders on the same 150 products and backtest, tuned on three earlier validation dates only. In short: lasting changes in sales are real and common, but reacting to them does not help, for any of the
time-series models we tried. What helps most is safety stock sized from the real spread of forecast errors rather
than a textbook formula. A better forecast, kept as it is, saves a little more in most but not all of the periods
and states we tested.

| 7 More time-series models | `07c` to `07i` | ARMA (with its AR and MA halves) and ARIMA as building blocks; the SARIMA family tuned one step at a time; VARIMA on the totals; Theta, Prophet and AutoTS; one comparison of them all; and a verdict on which is best, with ties, ranks and stock cost (`07i` runs after `11e`) |

The project reports 12 forecasting models: seasonal naive, Holt-Winters additive, Holt-Winters (damped, Box-Cox),
Theta, ARMA, ARIMA, SARIMA, SARIMAX, VARIMA (on the category totals), Prophet, AutoTS, and the combination (the plain
average of SARIMAX, the Box-Cox Holt-Winters and Theta).

`13_final_models` runs the final comparison of all 21 methods. `90_powerbi_export` and `91_dashboard_export` only

## How we kept it honest

Every forecast only sees data up to the day it is made, and models were tuned on earlier validation dates, never on
the test period. The tests and decision rules were written down before the results, and the numbers on the dashboard
are read from the saved result files.

## Limits

We only studied high-volume products, with sales added up over the stores, so stockouts in a single store are
hidden. The 726 products were chosen by looking at their whole sales history, including the test months, so the
selection is not strictly out-of-sample. Days with zero sales are kept as zero, so some may be stockouts rather than
real lack of demand. The final comparison uses a sample of 150 products, not all 726. The break detector misses small
changes. The costs of storage and of a lost sale are our assumptions, not Walmart's figures, and the dashboard marks
every result that depends on them. The data ends in 2016, so it says nothing about shopping after COVID.
